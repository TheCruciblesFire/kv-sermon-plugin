# Prose Normalization Workflow




Use this reference after a full or hybrid sermon draft is complete. This is an editorial second pass, not a new sermon-build pass. Preserve the sermon's theology, Main Idea, Goal, movement order, textual cautions, and applications unless a wording change is required for clarity.




## Narrative Boundary Rule


In full and hybrid manuscripts, paragraph boundaries represent changes in thought, not pauses in delivery. Shortness, emphasis, parallel wording, or a natural speaking pause do not by themselves justify a paragraph break.


Spoken pacing belongs primarily inside sentences and paragraphs. Paragraph breaks belong primarily to argument structure. When a claim, explanation, qualification, example, consequence, and application belong to one thought unit, keep them together as connected prose.


Use this governing presumption: **default to merge; isolate only when the line earns isolation** as Scripture, a major diagnostic question, a deliberate refrain, a structural transition, a true list, or a genuine climactic landing point.


During normalization, mentally strip away rhetorical line breaks first, rebuild the section around its argument units, and only then restore selected standalone lines for emphasis. If deleting blank lines would turn a section into coherent paragraphs without changing the actual argument, the manuscript probably has not completed normalization.


## Pass 1 — Complete the Sermon




Draft the sermon faithfully from the controlling passage or approved handoff. Do not interrupt content development to count paragraphs or calculate style ratios.




## Pass 2 — Normalize Section by Section




Work through the introduction, each movement, each application section, every transition, and the conclusion.




For each section:




1. Ignore sentence-pacing line breaks on the first read and identify the section's real argument units before deciding where paragraphs belong.
2. Identify adjacent ordinary one-sentence paragraphs that belong to the same argument unit, even when they are not repetitive or parallel. Look especially for a claim followed by explanation, qualification, consequence, illustration, or application.
3. Merge those units into natural spoken paragraphs, usually several sentences long. Do not keep a paragraph break merely because the next sentence could stand alone grammatically. Keep the break only when the thought actually turns or deliberate emphasis benefits from isolation.
4. Run a sentence-level cadence scan inside each paragraph. If three or more consecutive short simple sentences advance one thought, rewrite them into more natural spoken syntax using coordination, subordination, connective phrases, or sentence combination. Merely removing paragraph breaks is not enough.
5. Run a dedicated stacked-sequence scan: if three or more adjacent short declarative or imperative paragraphs appear in sequence, merge them into connected prose unless that sequence is the single intentional stacked refrain or climactic sequence for the section.
6. Preserve a standalone line only when its isolation clearly serves one of these functions:
   - Scripture quotation or reading cue;
   - heading or structural label;
   - true list;
   - direct response question;
   - deliberate refrain or call-and-response;
   - sharp contrast;
   - climactic statement or transition that gains force from isolation.
7. Permit no more than one intentionally stacked refrain or climactic short-line sequence in a major section. If another stacked sequence appears in that section, normalize it into prose.
8. If the same rhetorical device appears repeatedly across the section, normalize most occurrences back into prose. A device that is everywhere is no longer emphasis.
9. Only after the argument units are rebuilt should you restore selected standalone lines that clearly earn isolation. Re-read the section visually. If it resembles a speaking outline, slide deck, teleprompter stack, or sentence-per-line transcript, rewrite the whole section as connected prose before moving on.




## Normalize Syntax, Not Just Paragraph Breaks




A manuscript can be paragraph-dominant and still sound choppy. Do not treat several short independent sentences placed inside one paragraph as automatically normalized prose.




### Unwanted sentence-level choppiness




> He received the truth. He understood the truth. He practiced the truth. His discernment grew.




### Normalize




> As he received and understood the truth, he began to practice it, and that repeated obedience strengthened his discernment over time.




### Unwanted clipped exposition




> The truth does not change. Our understanding deepens. Our obedience deepens. Our lives begin to look different.




### Normalize




> The truth itself does not change, but as our understanding deepens and obedience follows, that same truth increasingly reshapes the way we live.




Use short simple sentences intentionally, not habitually. In ordinary exposition, vary sentence openings and sentence length, and let related clauses carry one another so the sermon sounds like connected speech rather than notes converted into paragraphs.




## Merge by Meaning, Not Mechanically




Combine sentences that belong to one thought unit. Do not create bloated paragraphs by joining unrelated ideas. A paragraph should normally develop one claim, explanation, application, or transition.




### Unwanted




> Peter begins with Christ.
>
> That matters.
>
> He does not begin with self-improvement.




### Normalize




> Peter begins with Christ, and that matters because he is not offering a generic self-improvement plan. He grounds Christian endurance in belonging to the suffering and victorious Jesus, so the command grows out of identity before it becomes application.




### Unwanted




> Peter does none of that.
>
> Pray clearly.
>
> Love deeply.
>
> Show hospitality.
>
> Serve one another.




### Normalize




> Peter does none of that. Instead, his response to the nearness of the end is strikingly ordinary and concrete: believers are to pray with clarity, love one another deeply, practice hospitality, and use what God has given them to serve. His eschatology produces present faithfulness rather than speculative distraction.




### Keep Standalone When It Truly Functions as Emphasis




A brief line such as **Christ is enough.** or **Do not turn away.** may remain isolated when it serves as a deliberate refrain or climactic landing point. Do not repeat that formatting throughout ordinary exposition.












### Unwanted argument-unit fragmentation




> We belong to him, and therefore our lives matter to him.
>
> Then Peter reaches verse 19, and the entire chapter comes to its natural landing point.
>
> Those who suffer according to God's will are to entrust themselves to a faithful Creator while doing what is good.
>
> There are two parts to that command, and Peter does not allow us to separate them.
>
> We entrust ourselves to God, and we continue doing good.




These sentences are not parallel, but they belong to one argument unit: identity, textual landing point, command, explanation, and summary. Normalize them into developed prose rather than preserving five paragraph breaks.




### Normalize




> We belong to God, and therefore our lives matter to him. That is why verse 19 becomes the chapter's natural landing point: those who suffer according to God's will are to entrust themselves to a faithful Creator while doing what is good. Peter holds both parts together. We entrust ourselves to God, and because we trust him, we continue doing good.




### Unwanted repeated declaratives




> Hostility does not give us permission to become hostile.
>
> Slander does not give us permission to slander.
>
> Rejection does not release us from love.
>
> Fear does not release us from obedience.
>
> Pressure does not release us from holiness.




### Normalize




> Suffering does not release us from Christian faithfulness. Hostility does not permit us to become hostile, slander does not justify slander in return, and rejection does not release us from love. Fear cannot become an excuse for disobedience, and pressure cannot become an excuse for abandoning holiness.




A stacked sequence like the first example may be retained only when it is the one deliberate climactic refrain for that section. Otherwise normalize it into prose.




## Final Editorial Question




Before delivery, ask one qualitative question for every major section:




> Does this read like a preacher speaking in connected thought, or like notes broken into lines or clipped sentences?




If the answer is the latter, rewrite the section before delivery.




Do not expose this internal normalization audit in the final sermon unless the user asks for editorial notes.
